How to Choose a Bathroom Remodeler

Published September 30, 2026 by Renue Home

Choose a bathroom remodeler by checking their credentials, comparing the same written scope across bids, and resolving exclusions before you sign. A lower total is useful only when you know what it buys. Use the worksheet below to compare the work behind each price.

This guide helps you prepare for a contractor conversation. The comparison worksheet is Renue Home's editorial tool. Consumer protection guidance comes from the FTC and EPA, linked where it applies. We have not inspected the contractors you may be considering or priced your project.

Give every remodeler the same starting brief

Write down what you want to replace and what should stay. A shower replacement, a new vanity and a full room remodel are different projects. Tell each company whether the toilet, shower and sink will remain in their current positions. Include room measurements if you have them, photos of the existing bathroom and any access restrictions.

Separate needs from preferences. You might need a working shower during the project but be flexible about the tile color. If easier access is your priority, describe the difficulty you are trying to solve and ask how the proposed design addresses it. A product labeled accessible does not explain whether it fits your room or your needs.

Ask each bidder to list assumptions made before an on-site inspection. Keep a copy of the brief so you can tell whether a revised proposal changes the scope or simply changes the price.

Check the business and the people doing the work

The FTC recommends checking contractor licensing with your state or county, asking for proof of insurance and obtaining multiple written estimates. Ask which business will sign the contract and which trades will perform the work. Match the business name on the proposal to the documents you check.

Ask for examples of projects similar to yours. A portfolio of finished bathrooms is useful, but your questions should cover the process too. Who supervised the work, how were unexpected problems priced, and who responded after the final payment? Ask references those questions instead of relying only on the appearance of the finished room.

For a home built before 1978, EPA recommends a lead-safe certified contractor for renovation work. Raise the age of the home before demolition is scheduled.

Compare scope before comparing totals

Read every proposal using the same categories. The worksheet deliberately leaves prices blank. It is a way to compare your actual bids, not a national price estimate or a contractor score. Print this page, or copy the rows into your own notes, and ask each bidder to fill the gaps.

Resolve the unanswered questions

  • Who will be my day-to-day contact, and who can approve a change?
  • Which products are specified by model, and which are allowances?
  • What preparation and waterproofing are included behind the finished surfaces?
  • Who checks the local permit and inspection requirements?
  • What happens if demolition exposes damage outside the quoted scope?
  • When will the bathroom be unusable, and what could change that schedule?
  • How will you document a change in price before extra work begins?
  • Who handles a leak, installation defect or product warranty claim after completion?

Read the agreement before paying

The FTC advises homeowners to get a written agreement, avoid paying the full project price in advance and check local rules on deposits. There is no single deposit percentage that this guide can apply nationwide. Read the FTC's contract and payment guidance.

Compare the agreement to the worksheet. If a promised item is missing, ask for it to be added. A friendly conversation does not resolve an exclusion in writing. Keep the proposal, product selections, change approvals and completion notes together so both sides can refer to the same record.

Know when to slow down

Pressure to decide immediately, demands for full payment before work and refusal to document the scope are reasons to pause. The FTC identifies upfront-payment demands and high-pressure sales as common scam warnings. A very low bid also deserves questions, but price alone does not establish that a contractor is dishonest.

If two bids remain hard to compare, ask each company to revise its scope using the same worksheet. You may decide that paying more for a clearly specified project is preferable to an attractive total with unresolved exclusions. The decision belongs to you.
